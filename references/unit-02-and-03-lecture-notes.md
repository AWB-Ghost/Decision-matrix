## Unit 2 — How to handle the eight combinations
Instructor says classic Eisenhower matrix has two factors: urgent and important. He introduces a third factor: **time taken**. 'Does the task take more than 5 minutes?' The answer is No if **5 minutes or less**, Yes if **more than 5 minutes**. Eight combinations result.

1. **Neither urgent nor important, <=5 minutes:** Do it and be done, such as a brief restorative break. Do not feel bad about a short nonproductive activity. The instructor mentions Pomodoro 25-minute work/5-minute break intervals and a longer break after four cycles; this is a teaching example, **not** an app timer requirement.
2. **Neither urgent nor important, >5 minutes:** Optional leisure, Netflix, reels, friends; don't forbid, but do it after important tasks for the day have been addressed. It is an elective allocation.
3. **Not urgent, important, <=5 minutes:** Do it now; examples short call to parents. Meaningful quick tasks should not be postponed just because they are not urgent.
4. **Not urgent, important, >5 minutes:** Schedule and make part of routine. Examples exercise, reading, writing, hobbies, mental wellness, meditation. Repeated neglect of this quadrant is a long-term loss.
5. **Urgent, not important, <=5 minutes:** Get it done so that small unresolved tasks don't distract from other priorities.
6. **Urgent, not important, >5 minutes:** If occasional, get it done when possible after important work; if regular, deliberately reserve an `urgent allocation` buffer. The instructor gives a rhythm of repeated interruptions every four hours as an example; buffer duration should be based on the person's own observed data (15, 30, 45 or 60 minutes), not a preset.
7. **Urgent, important, <=5 minutes:** Do it now and be done.
8. **Urgent, important, >5 minutes:** Attend as soon as reasonably possible (half-hour, hour, two hours, depending on urgency). Do not blindly drop everything when it only *seems* urgent; true crises may need immediate attention.

Instructor concludes: a diligent student records and classifies their day over a 10-day period. The 8 cases provide a **playbook for making task decisions**, not a substitute for measuring where time went.

## Unit 3 — How to use the time allocation exercise sheet
- The sheet shows the hours of the day. A user may choose **1 hour or 30 minutes**; teacher explicitly suggests 1-hour because the exercise itself requires effort.
- For every slot, write the activity you actually did (the majority task if multiple), and whether it was urgent and important. The definitions are **personal**. A client call can be considered important by a company but not by you for this exercise.
- The right-hand matrix updates as entries are added; the result is the actual daily mix of the four urgent/important combinations.
- At the end of each day ask **'How do you feel the day went?'** Answer **Yes or No** to whether you felt happy; optionally write *why*.
- Over **10 days**, seek patterns between days you were happy and the mix. Over **30 days**, there may be stronger clues. As an example (not a rule), the instructor mentions some people might be satisfied on days with 40–50% important/not-urgent work and unsatisfied when urgent activity dominates. Never treat this as an app goal or target.
- After gathering evidence, identify activities **under your control** and place those into deliberate slots (exercise, reading, sleeping, watching television can all be deliberate). Leave open-ended spaces for unpredictable boss/colleague/family demands.
- The core outcome is **feeling more in control of time**, not compulsory all-day productivity. Consistent measurement and thoughtful allocation come first.
